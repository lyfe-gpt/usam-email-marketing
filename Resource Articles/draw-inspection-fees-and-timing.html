Construction draws

Every draw gets inspected first

On a build or a heavy rehab, money is released against completed work. Someone verifies that work before each release, and that step is usually what decides whether your schedule holds.

Apply now

What the inspector confirms

Percentage of completion. The draw inspector checks that the line items you billed for are done to the stage you say they are.

Not quality, and not code compliance. That is the municipal building inspector, a separate visit entirely.

What it costs and who pays

A per-draw fee, paid by you, usually deducted from the draw itself rather than billed separately. It is a third-party cost, not a lender margin line.

So more draws means more inspection fees. Five draws cost more in inspections than three. Against that, fewer draws means carrying more of the work on your own cash between releases. Decide that trade at closing, not at draw three.

The four things that stall a draw

Billing ahead of the work. The most common cause. Bill 60% and the inspector finds 45%, and the draw funds at 45%. You cover the gap.

Unreleased liens. A sub who has not signed off can hold the release even when the work is done. Scope drift. Work that is not on the approved budget does not draw against it, however necessary it turned out to be.

Scheduling. The inspection has to be booked, attended, and reported. That is real calendar time, and it repeats every draw.

How to keep draws moving

Bill slightly behind where you actually are. Keep lien releases current as you go rather than at the end. Ask for the inspection as the work closes out, not after.

Then build the inspection cycle into your schedule from the start. Our ground-up terms run 12 to 24 months, and every stalled draw eats into that. Terms are set in underwriting. Not a commitment to lend.
